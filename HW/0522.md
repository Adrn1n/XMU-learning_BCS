This paper by Lee et al. (2022) presents an explainable consciousness indicator (ECI) using deep learning to quantify arousal and awareness in altered states of consciousness (e.g., sleep, anesthesia, disorders of consciousness). The method combines transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and electroencephalography (EEG) with a convolutional neural network (CNN) and layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) for interpretability.

- Feasibility
	The study demonstrates that deep learning can successfully decode and quantify consciousness from EEG data. The ECI, a 2D metric (arousal vs. awareness), accurately distinguishes between low/high arousal and awareness across physiological, pharmacological, and pathological conditions. It correlates strongly with the established perturbational complexity index (PCI) and generalizes to resting-state EEG without TMS. This shows that deep learning models, when trained on appropriate neurophysiological data, can provide a reliable, objective, and quantitative measure of consciousness.
- Challenges
	1. Data limitations: Small sample sizes per condition may limit generalizability; larger cohorts are needed for clinical validation.
	2. Interpretability vs. performance trade-off: While LRP reveals that the parietal region is most relevant for classification, the CNN’s internal representations remain complex and not fully explainable.
	3. Practical constraints: Real-time application and minimal-trial calculation are promising but require further testing in clinical settings.
	4. Task specificity: The model learns to classify predefined states (low/high arousal/awareness) but does not directly measure “consciousness” as a unified construct; it reflects correlates rather than the phenomenon itself.
	5. Technical issues: TMS-EEG data may contain sensory artifacts despite noise-masking; resting-state EEG without TMS is less reliable but still feasible.

Overall, the paper shows that combining brain signals (EEG) with deep learning is a feasible approach to quantify consciousness, but challenges remain in interpretability, scalability, and clinical translation.
